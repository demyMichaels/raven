Waves · Intuition First

The Note You Can Hear

A tuning fork hums. You can hear it — a clear, piercing note near the top of a soprano's range.

But what is actually happening? The prongs are vibrating back and forth 1300 times every second. Each vibration is so fast you can't see it, but your ear can hear the result.

That number — 1300 per second — is what we're about to make sense of. And it has a surprise: the time for one vibration is a number you'd never guess.

The Passage

Read it once. Don't solve it yet.

An oscillator vibrates with a frequency of 1.30 × 10³ Hz. Calculate (i) the period of oscillation, (ii) the number of complete oscillations it makes in 4.0 s.

Most students will reach for a calculator. Don't. First answer this:

What does 1.30 × 10³ Hz actually sound like? If you can't place this number in the real world, the arithmetic won't teach you anything.

Build the Anchor

What is 1.30 × 10³ Hz?

1300 vibrations every second.
That's a high musical note — roughly E6, near the top of a soprano's range. About 3× higher than concert A (440 Hz).

So read it out loud, slowly:

"One thousand three hundred wiggles per second."

Now — how long does one wiggle take? On the next screen, you'll hear it and see it.

Now Do the Maths

T = 1/f and N = f × t

Period is the inverse of frequency. Number of oscillations is frequency × time.

(i) Period

f1.30 × 10³ Hz
T = 1/f1 / (1.30 × 10³)
= (1/1.30) × 10⁻³
T7.7 × 10⁻⁴ s

(ii) Number of oscillations in 4.0 s

N = f × t1.30 × 10³ × 4.0
= 5.2 × 10³
N5200 oscillations
Answer
Correct Answer
T = 7.7 × 10⁻⁴ s
N = 5.2 × 10³ oscillations

One vibration ≈ 0.77 ms · 5200 vibrations in 4 seconds

Notice the pattern: high frequency means short period. The numbers move in opposite directions. That inverse relationship is the whole point — and it's why a 1300 Hz note feels "fast" to your ear: each cycle is over in under a millisecond.

Lock It In

What you actually learned

  • ✓Hz means "per second." 1.30 × 10³ Hz = 1300 vibrations every second.
  • ✓Period is the inverse of frequency. T = 1/f. High f → tiny T.
  • ✓0.77 ms is the anchor. A camera flash. Something you can feel, not just compute.
  • ✓You heard it — and saw it. Higher frequencies squeeze more cycles into the same window.

Next time you meet a frequency, don't jump to T = 1/f. First ask: can I hear this? Is it fast or slow compared to my heartbeat? Then the arithmetic has somewhere to land.
